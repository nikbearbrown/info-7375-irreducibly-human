## Claims to examine

Do not convert statements such as AI always outperforming a human, never reasoning causally, being unable to report uncertainty, or all future machines lacking embodied skills into uncontested technical laws. Distinguish task performance, conditions of measurement, theoretical assumptions, and philosophical arguments. Students can disagree with the book when they locate the claim and provide reasoning and evidence.

An output that sounds empathic does not establish feelings or a relationship. That distinction does not justify an unmeasured claim that every AI-assisted interaction harms people. A classroom simulation is not a human-participant study. The causal chapter's illustrative clinical story includes composite and verification qualifications; preserve them. Causal identification depends on assumptions and available evidence, not a blanket ban on machines implementing causal methods.

The independence assumptions behind voting examples are explicit. A group of model personas is not automatically independent; actual people may also share sources and errors. Weighted decision scores reflect chosen values rather than proving what is right.

## Research boundary

The local research handoff and worksheet describe pending independent coding and rubric ratification for ability/skill mappings; see the public [research-boundary summary](research-boundary.md). The course does not perform or sign that research protocol. Lesson 14 uses synthetic elements and clearly labeled hypothetical mappings. Do not call them rubric-v2-frozen, recompute production research as though a freeze exists, or infer employment loss from a task-exposure score. Keep classroom grading separate from the research coding rubric.

## Computational Skepticism

Read [Chapter 1, “What I mean by skepticism”](../../info-7375-computational-skepticism-for-ai/chapters/01-the-skeptics-toolkit.md). Apply its concrete falsifier practice in Lessons 2, 7, and 9. A claim should name what observation could count against it and how that observation would be checked. The companion has documented editorial conflict blocks; those are not instructions to students.

## Conducting AI

Read [Chapter 9, “The Handoff as the Unit of Risk”](../../info-7375-conducting-ai/chapters/09-tool-orchestration-part-2.md). Apply in Lessons 6 and 12: specify what passes between people or tools, the trust asserted, and the evidence that earns it. A summary or a generated message cannot create stakeholder consent.

## Prompt Engineering for Generative AI

Read [Lesson 2, prompt contracts and evaluation](../../info-7375-prompt-engineering-for-generative-ai/lessons/02-prompt-contracts-and-evaluation/docs/en.md) as optional preparation for the structured records used here. Specify inputs, output fields, and validation rules; test the meaning separately from format. Claude Code remains the shared tool.

## Branding and AI

Read [Chapter 18, Portfolio as Product](../../info-7375-branding-and-ai/chapters/18-portfolio-as-product.md), distinguishing audience-facing explanation from GitHub's technical evidence. Lesson 14 makes the limits of a quantitative claim readable and traceable. A video or public deployment is not required.
